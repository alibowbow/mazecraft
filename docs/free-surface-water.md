# Vertical free-surface water

The default **물 흐름** view simulates a vertical maze cross-section. Each water particle carries a fixed area. Gravity, area-normalized density constraints, viscosity and swept wall sliding produce falling jets, branches and pools within a maze cell. No solution path, arrival schedule or cell-fill animation drives the water boundary.

- `freeSurface/layout.ts` builds symmetric solid AABBs from the actual maze, a tapered top funnel and a bottom outlet. The funnel's collision walls and visible bowl share the same dimensions; water enters above its mouth and falls through its neck. Inactive cells remain inaccessible. The two visual endpoints use the topmost/bottommost active row.
- `solver.ts` integrates at 120 Hz independently of display quality. A spatial hash bounds neighbour searches; swept AABB movement prevents thin-wall tunnelling. Three position-based density projections oppose compression using each particle's area. This remains an approximate visual liquid, not calibrated CFD or a volumetric 3D incompressible solver.
- `fluid.worker.ts` runs bounded batches off the UI thread. The runtime allows one request in flight and starts the next batch on completion when physical time is owed, independently of the next render frame. Display demand requests the next completed snapshot without waiting for catch-up to finish; intermediate batches return small acknowledgements without particle copies or diagnostic scans. One final catch-up refinement preserves the newest completed state. Two reusable transfer-buffer pairs circulate between renderer and Worker, including pause/restart recycling. UI counters use the displayed state. It rejects stale generations after restart, drops queued/late snapshots while paused, and falls back to the same solver if initial Worker creation/loading fails. A hidden page accumulates no catch-up debt.
- `renderer.ts` reconstructs a continuous water boundary from velocity-aligned optical footprints. Two wall-aware smoothing passes remove particle-sized bumps. Interior transmission and broad reflections come from the continuous silhouette, while a narrow meniscus highlights the outer boundary; individual density peaks receive no separate lighting. Appearance presets do not alter physics. Pan, zoom and fit also work while paused.
- **물 흐름** and **3D 수면** now share one particle runtime, Worker and snapshot. Changing the view preserves the water, time, pause state and supply state. `presentation3d.ts` projects the continuous water field onto an actual board with extruded maze walls; the camera can orbit, pan and zoom. This is a 3D presentation of the same vertical 2D fluid cross-section, not a separate volumetric fluid solver. The previous hydraulic renderer and Blender atlas modules remain in the repository but are no longer the dialog's 3D runtime.

## Controls and accounting

**공급 끄기** stops admitting new water while existing water continues to move. **일시정지** freezes simulation and rendering; **처음부터** creates a new generation and resets all counters and inlet state. Speed changes only the number of fixed simulation steps.

Both views provide the same controls. **2D 물 흐름** and **3D 수면** are always-visible pressed buttons, including on narrow phones and short landscape screens. In 2D, drag pans; in 3D, drag orbits and Shift-drag pans. A two-finger gesture pans and zooms in either view. The changing footer narration and outlet notification have been removed; source backpressure remains available in diagnostics without repeated messages.

The 3D view uses a direct-grab quaternion trackball: the grabbed side follows the finger, including from the back or after rolling the board. Rotation has no yaw/pitch limits or pole flip. Panning follows the current camera plane; wheel/pinch zoom preserves the screen anchor. The original initial fit stays constant during rotation instead of automatically changing size. Zoom can reach 0.25× to fit a rolled board; **화면 맞춤**, double-click, Home or 0 restores the initial view. Camera events coalesce into one draw per animation frame, shared with incoming simulation rendering.

Visible, labelled color buttons offer colorless water, six tints and a native custom-color picker. **투명 물** uses equal RGB absorption, no colored scattering, neutral reflections and 82% optical-composite strength; transmission still shows the backing. **청록** preserves the former default's exact blue-green absorption/scattering and 58% composite strength. Other tints use channel-dependent absorption at 68% strength. These strengths mix the complete optical result with the backing; they are not physical material opacity. Surface styles also use visible pressed buttons. Appearance changes reuse the same Worker, particles, displayed time, supply and pause state in both views.

Diagnostics use cell squared for 2D water area and cells/second for velocity. An outlet crossing counts each particle once. Discharged particles remain visible below the outlet until leaving the visual domain; their area is already counted as discharged and is excluded from stored area. Other exits are counted separately. Thus `injected = stored + discharged + escaped`.

The deterministic capacity is capped at 18,000 particles. A crowded source or full particle budget throttles actual admission rather than losing water or inventing injection. Long and winding mazes may take substantially longer to fill; under load the bounded work queue slows simulation relative to wall time instead of increasing the integration step or blocking the interface. Renderer quality affects pixels only.

## Earlier source and clock correction

The source checks all six emission lanes before declaring backpressure. The previous implementation stopped at one occupied lane and discarded its remaining supply budget, even when other lanes were free. Launch speed now matches the requested particles per second and lane spacing, capped by the existing maximum velocity. This source correction preserved nominal supply rate, particle area, gravity, collision handling and pressure relaxation. A fully blocked nozzle retains at most one pending particle; turning supply off clears that remainder. The subsequent density correction below changes pressure relaxation, so a physically crowded funnel can admit fewer particles while filling its available space sooner.

A separate clock defect previously counted at most 50 ms per render frame. At 10 fps that discarded half of the physical elapsed time. The Worker path now counts normal frame delays up to 250 ms and drains a bounded 500 ms backlog in batches of at most twelve 1/120-second steps. Rendering does not need to finish between every physics batch. This recovers ordinary slow-frame time without accumulating long hidden-tab delays or unbounded catch-up work. The main-thread fallback retains its smaller per-frame work limit.

In a deterministic closed-cell fixture after one simulated second, admitted particles increased from 55 to 90, particles inside the cell from 28 to 48, and slow pooled particles from 11 to 29. The slow-particle surface-height estimate rose from 0.409 to 0.740 cell; this excludes the inlet and falling jet. In the seeded 6×6 maze, stored particle area after five simulated seconds increased from 5.72 to 9.62 cell². These are controlled visual-solver measurements, not calibrated physical-volume predictions or a universal fill-time guarantee.

A before/after headless Chromium measurement in the same container used about nine wall-clock seconds per case, with no concurrent test load. Physical progress as a fraction of elapsed wall time improved as follows:

| Maze / graphics quality | Before | After |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 6×6 / low | 69.3% | 98.2% |
| 6×6 / high | 59.2% | 98.0% |
| 24×24 / low | 61.3% | 94.8% |
| 24×24 / high | 59.0% | 78.5% |

The corrected source also puts more particles into the scene during that time. The 24×24 high-quality case had 2.56 times as many particles after the fix and remained limited by software rendering/solver work. These results do not promise real-time performance on every device or maze.

## Faster default supply and solver cost

The subsequent default-supply change requests three times the earlier nominal rate, capped at 360 particles per second: 330/s for 6×6 and 360/s for 8×8. Emission speed accounts for each lane's discrete fixed-step revisit interval, so the preceding drop clears the nozzle when that lane is next eligible. Existing six-lane spacing, gravity, maximum velocity, wall collision, density constraints and crowded-source backpressure remain in effect. The visible funnel and real admission still agree; no water is added directly into receiving cells.

Measured against the preceding revision with the same seed, funnel and 1× simulation, actual particles inside the maze after one second increased from 48 to 182 for 6×6 and from 54 to 168 for 8×8. In receiving-basin fixtures with the real funnel, half-height pooling time fell from 3.70 to 1.57 seconds for 6×6 and from 5.10 to 1.93 seconds for 8×8. The criterion requires at least twelve slow particles above half height away from the falling jet, so it does not count the first high droplet as a filled pool. These are deterministic fixture measurements; route geometry and device load still affect perceived filling time.

The hot loop now stops scanning a descending hash bucket once only already-visited particle indices remain. Reusable Float64 pair geometry avoids recalculating the same distance/kernel in the density and correction loops; viscosity no longer needs per-pair callbacks. Squared-length checks avoid square roots when velocity/displacement are already within their limits. Pair visibility is still rebuilt after positional corrections. In fixed-count, source-off tests of 120 steps (median of five runs in the same Node container), CPU time fell from 408 to 301 ms for 1,000 particles and from 1,198 to 893 ms for 3,000 particles, about 25–26%. The 3,000-particle maximum position difference was 0.0000024 cell; particle counts and accounting were unchanged. Additional reusable pair geometry costs 32 bytes per cached pair.

The following optimization keeps the same equations, 120 Hz, three pressure iterations and particle capacity. A static fine-grid wall index, packed wall bounds, and conservative empty-cell/pair masks avoid repeated broad-phase and intersection work; potentially intersecting paths still use exact wall tests. Exact-zero moves do no collision work. Against `7ace940`, 120-step fixed-count comparisons at 1,000/3,000/8,000 particles preserve every output position and velocity bit-for-bit while reducing CPU time about 29–34%. Interleaved real 6×6/8×8/16×16 maze measurements after ten simulated seconds showed 29–37% less solver CPU. The additional static index used 5.42 MB for a 150×150 layout and initialized in 119 ms in this container. More complex Verlet and cell-centric variants were measured and rejected because their gains were inconsistent.

Snapshot generation reuses both particle arrays and the wet-cell bitmap, with one square root for maximum velocity rather than one per particle. A 3,000-particle, 1,000-snapshot benchmark fell from 118.86 to 30.06 ms; these are snapshot-only timings, not whole-frame gains. In 120 displayed-frame transport tests, binary array creation fell from 242 arrays to at most four. After the initial ring allocation, particle buffers are transferred back and reused rather than continually allocated and discarded.

## Pool compression and unsupported sheets

Particle accounting alone did not ensure that the visible pool occupied its reported area. The previous double-density relaxation targeted an arbitrary neighbour count and allowed pair distances below the particle diameter. In captured 6×6 and 24×24 mazes, fully settled cells contained about 1.50–1.61 times their actual wall-excluded area. New supply therefore compressed existing water before raising the surface.

After the correction, the same two settled cells contain about 1.07 times their wall-excluded area (49 particles instead of 69 and 74). At about ten simulated seconds the 24×24 scene has already overflowed into the next left branch. This checks actual Worker particle positions rather than assuming that a larger rendered silhouette means more water.

The solver now uses an area-normalized 2D poly6 kernel and three position-based density projections. Each particle contributes its accounting area; the constraint only opposes density above the reference density. The small negative-pressure attraction in the old solver is removed, so sparse water is not pulled into an unsupported sheet. Contact separation supports particles where a wall truncates the density kernel. Under-relaxation and the existing bounded swept displacement preserve stability. The approach follows the density-constraint principle of [Macklin and Müller's Position Based Fluids](https://mmacklin.com/pbf_slides.pdf), adapted independently to this two-dimensional visual solver.

Thin walls now block neighbour pressure and viscosity as well as movement. Previously the 0.294-cell interaction radius could connect particles across a 0.10-cell wall. A short segment/AABB visibility check excludes those pairs. Accepted pairs are stored in a reusable typed array and shared by density and displacement passes, avoiding repeated hash searches in the same iteration. Neighbours are rebuilt after each positional iteration and before viscosity; moved particles never reuse an earlier iteration's visibility.

In a fixed-count test, 300 particles were admitted into a one-cell-wide open-top tank, then supply was stopped and the water settled for six simulated seconds. Its 5% upper-surface quantile rose from 4.672 to 5.917 cells above the floor, against the area/clear-width expectation of 6.207 cells (75.3% to 95.3%). Median depth improved from 68.0% to 93.5% of its expected value. Two additional shallow/wide fixtures and another two seconds without supply verify that this is occupied-area preservation rather than a transient inlet jet. In the same Node/container benchmark, settling 1,000 particles for six simulated seconds took 4.553 seconds before and 3.130 seconds after the change, about 31% less CPU time. Timings depend on hardware; the solver remains an approximation, not perfectly incompressible.

A single still image of water across a downward opening cannot distinguish transient flow from persistent hanging water. Regression fixtures therefore also stop supply and verify that an unsupported sheet falls through the opening, and that water in separate wall-isolated chambers cannot exchange momentum. Optical footprints still smooth close droplets into a continuous jet; that display field is not a physical volume measurement. The solver does not model trapped-air pressure, wetting/contact angles or a full three-dimensional liquid.

A further gravity audit compared `7ace940` with the optimized solver: free-fall groups follow g=12 in center-of-mass position/velocity, an unsupported sheet fully drops through its opening, and a settled source-off tank does not gain height or kinetic energy over four further simulated seconds. Metrics matched exactly. Residual numerical jitter remains (about 0.136 cells/s RMS in the settled fixture), and optical footprints can visually connect nearby disconnected drops. No global upward-velocity clamp, gravity change or settling freeze is used; pressure-driven return flow remains possible.

## Optical reconstruction

Fast optical footprints stretch along the snapshot velocity by at most 2×. This bridges close samples in a jet without changing particle positions, collision radii, or water accounting. A conservative world-space solid mask rejects footprints and smoothing samples across walls, including when the camera zooms out. Its one-byte texels are capped at 4096 per axis. All added textures and render targets are released on close.

The 3D field adds 0.42 cells of visual padding around the solver domain so a falling particle's optical footprint is not cut flat at the board edge. This padding does not extend the physical simulation or change discharge accounting.

The water composites against a lightly etched backing, with shallow optical refraction and restrained reflection. The backing remains visible through pools. Colorless water uses neutral optical coefficients; colored profiles additionally tint transmission. The coverage field is only an optical surface estimate, not a measurement of water volume. Transmission, reflection, and meniscus lighting are visual approximations. The subtle moving light band uses accepted simulation time and is gated by local speed; pause freezes it along with the fluid. No independent animation clock is introduced.

The 3D texture additionally takes visual principles from the user's [River Surfer](https://riversurfer.daropo.chatgpt.site), whose live water and deployed shaders were inspected: broad waves with finer ripple detail, view-angle Fresnel reflection, refracted backing and narrow sunlight highlights. MazeCraft implements four analytic slope bands, derivative-filtered fine detail, neutral environment reflection and a bounded GGX light lobe in its existing optical pass. The orthographic camera supplies the actual board-to-eye direction, so highlights respond to orbiting. Local measured speed gates agitation; the current field stores scalar speed, so wave directions are deliberately varied rather than claimed to reconstruct a local velocity vector. This is a normal/reflection treatment of the same surface, not geometric ocean waves, FFT simulation, extra reflection/refraction scene passes or a new fluid engine. The reference Site is unchanged.

## Rendering work

Density reconstruction and both smoothing passes are cached until particles, field coordinates or resolution change. Color/style changes and 3D orbiting only redraw the optical composite; orbiting needs the updated view direction for reflection and retains the cached density/smoothing field. Supply indicators do not invalidate density. Reset/empty snapshots and mode changes invalidate the field, so old water cannot remain cached. Snapshot uploads use typed-array bulk copies and upload only live particle ranges.

The existing full-quality configuration remains: the field uses 75% of the drawing-buffer dimensions, with a 1280/high or 960/low maximum edge; final output is capped at 1.5 DPR/1.6 million pixels for high quality and 1 DPR/0.9 million pixels for low quality. **Automatic resolution reduction has been removed.** Sustained slow frames retain the selected output size, field size, wave bands, shader detail, particle capacity and physics step. Camera/style/color changes never advance simulation time.

Static horizontal/vertical wall-visibility weights are rendered once per field projection or resize. Dynamic filtering reads those exact binary predicates rather than resampling the same walls: 28 texture fetches per pixel across two passes become 12, with unchanged filter coefficients. A conservative clearance mask can prove a splat ray is wall-free; near walls the original eight visibility samples remain. Density and intermediate filtering use RG8 instead of RGBA8, retaining the same R/G precision. Filters clear the full prior target and shade only a conservative wet bounding box, including the maximum splat/filter support and texel margin; omitted density/coverage values are exactly zero. No target dimensions are reduced.

In a headless Chromium software-WebGL comparison at fixed 1200×800 output and 900×600 field resolution, 8×8/1,800-particle and 24×24/8,000-particle screenshots matched `7ace940` in every pixel, in both 2D and the initial 3D view. All four high-DPR comparisons also matched exactly at 1549×1032 output and 1162×775 field resolution. The scissor is set in render-target texels, independent of canvas DPR. GPU readback-synchronized filter timings fell roughly 60–70%; end-to-end performance also depends on particle density, hardware and the optical pass. These are measured subsystem gains, not a promise of a particular device frame rate.

A separate seeded 8×8 high-quality 3D app run measured twelve wall-clock seconds after warmup. Actual water-field updates increased from 9.92 to 12.83 per second, about 29%, at identical 1140×389 output and 855×292 field dimensions. Physical progress increased from 91.3% to 99.1% of elapsed time, ending with 2,027 versus 2,066 particles. This counts rendered water updates, not requestAnimationFrame callbacks that may not draw; it is one software-WebGL workload rather than a hardware FPS guarantee.
